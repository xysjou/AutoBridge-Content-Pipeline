Roll-on/roll-off (RoRo) lets a drivable vehicle move under its own power on and off a car carrier, avoiding crane lifting. It is the natural mode for batches of new or used cars, pickups and light commercials that can be driven safely. The risk in RoRo is not the concept but the handover: between your depot, the terminal, the carrier and the vessel there are several points where condition, documents and responsibility change. This page maps that sequence for a Chinese export port and flags what must be confirmed per voyage rather than assumed.

## The end-to-end RoRo sequence

1. **Booking** — space is reserved with the car-carrier line or its booking agent for a specific vessel/voyage and destination; the carrier confirms acceptance (drivable units, dimensions, any EV/alternative-fuel conditions).
2. **Documentation & customs** — export declaration, licence/quota paperwork, booking note and the unit list (VIN, make/model, dimensions, condition) are completed so the vehicle is customs-released before the terminal cut-off.
3. **Delivery to terminal** — units are driven or trucked into the terminal within the agreed delivery window and enter the terminal yard.
4. **Receipt & joint inspection** — at handover, the receiving party visually checks each unit (exterior, interior, mileage/fuel, accessories) against the condition record; discrepancies are noted before the vehicle enters the yard.
5. **Stowage planning & loading** — terminal tally and the vessel plan assign deck position; drivers move vehicles aboard and secure them under the carrier's lashing plan.
6. **B/L and sailing** — the bill of lading (RoRo waybill/B/L) is issued against the received units; the vessel sails and the discharge-port party takes over.

## Where liability actually transfers

China's port cargo-operation rules require the port operator and the cargo receiver to inspect on delivery and, where damage or shortage is found, draw up a cargo record on the spot; if the receiver raises no quantity/quality objection at handover, delivery is presumed made unless later evidence proves otherwise. In practice this makes the **joint condition inspection at terminal receipt** the exporter's most important defensive moment: a scratch not recorded before the vehicle enters the yard is very hard to attribute afterward.

The industry operating standard for finished-vehicle multimodal RoRo (JT/T 1194) sets out tally, lashing-check and handover procedures — including checking that lashings are tight and that wheel chocks are placed/removed without touching the body beyond tyres. It defines a professional process; it is not a universal substitute for a named carrier's voyage instructions.

## Carrier-specific items you cannot standardise

> Fuel level on handover, tyre pressure, lashing points and tie-down method/angle, orientation on deck, acceptable state of charge for electrified vehicles, booking cut-off and terminal charges all vary by port, terminal and voyage, and several major RoRo carriers publish their own acceptance terms. Confirm each in writing for the actual booking; this guide deliberately states no universal numeric values.

| Item | Who sets it | What to obtain |
|---|---|---|

## A defensible pre-delivery checklist (recommended practice)

- Photograph/video each unit (four sides, interior, odometer/fuel display, VIN plate) before the gate.
- Match the VIN list to booking and customs documents one-to-one; a VIN error can void export paperwork.
- Remove personal items and loose accessories that move in transit; record what stays with the vehicle.
- Carry the release documents so the gate accepts the unit; arrive inside the delivery window.
- Stay for, or have the agent report, the joint receipt inspection and keep the signed condition record.
- For electrified units, follow the carrier's stated state-of-charge and dangerous-goods requirements (see the IMDG/UN classification guide) rather than a generic percentage.

## RoRo versus the alternatives

Containerised shipping suits non-drivable, high-value or mixed-cargo units and gives enclosure, but introduces loading/securing work and dimension limits. RoRo suits drivable batches and port-to-port movement on car-carrier schedules. Choice depends on the unit, destination port infrastructure, schedule and the carrier's current acceptance — compare both for the actual route before committing, and keep freight costs as quoted figures rather than assumptions.

## FAQ
**Does RoRo require the vehicle to be drivable?**

Generally yes — RoRo moves units under their own power; non-drivable or damaged units may need special handling or container shipping, subject to carrier acceptance.

**Is there a standard fuel level for handover?**

No universal figure — fuel-on-handover is set by the carrier/terminal for the voyage; confirm it in writing.

**Who is responsible for a scratch found at destination?**

Liability hinges on the condition record at terminal receipt and the carrier terms; a complete joint inspection at handover is the exporter’s key evidence.

**Are lashing rules the same for every car carrier?**

No — lashing and stowage are carrier/vessel-specific; follow the named carrier plan, not a generic standard.

**Can EVs ship by RoRo normally?**

Subject to the carrier’s electrified-vehicle and dangerous-goods requirements and the correct UN/IMDG classification; confirm before booking.
