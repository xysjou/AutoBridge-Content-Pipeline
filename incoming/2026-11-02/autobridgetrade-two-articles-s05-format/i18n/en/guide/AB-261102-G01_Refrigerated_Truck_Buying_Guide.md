A refrigerated truck can look convincing in a quotation and still be wrong for the job. The chassis may carry the insulated body but leave insufficient cargo capacity. The refrigeration unit may have a strong headline rating under conditions unlike the buyer’s route. A long body may provide space that cannot be filled without obstructing airflow. The useful buying question is therefore not simply which chassis or freezer is cheapest. It is whether the completed vehicle can carry the intended goods, maintain their required conditions and be supported where it will work. The method below helps procurement teams build that decision without inventing a universal truck specification.

## Write the route and cargo brief before asking for prices

Describe the products, their required transport conditions, packaging, loading temperature, typical cargo mass and the busiest delivery pattern. Include the number and duration of stops, loading access, expected ambient conditions and whether the vehicle waits with its engine off. Distinguish chilled deliveries from frozen work and single-temperature loads from loads needing separate compartments. The cargo owner’s instructions and applicable destination requirements determine the actual temperature brief; a generic internet temperature table is not a substitute. Thermo King’s guidance connects refrigeration performance to loading condition, airflow and door-opening practices, so these details belong in the buying specification rather than a conversation after delivery. ## Check completed-vehicle payload, not an unfinished chassis number

Ask the supplier to state gross-vehicle and axle ratings, completed-vehicle mass and the remaining permitted payload for the exact body and options. The insulated body, refrigeration equipment, tail lift and other fitted items all need to be accounted for in the completed configuration. A chassis allowance labelled for “body and payload” must not be represented as cargo capacity after conversion. Also request evidence that the intended load distribution fits the relevant axle limits. Do not publish a generic payload just because two trucks share a cab or body length. The buyer needs the completed vehicle’s documents and, where appropriate, a weighing record, not an estimate copied from a base-chassis advertisement.

## Choose the body around the goods and the loading method

Truck Home’s technical articles describe the chassis, insulated body and refrigeration unit as interdependent parts. Use that relationship when comparing offers. Ask for usable internal dimensions, door openings, floor layout, insulation information and the positions of evaporators and air-return paths. A nominal external body length says little about pallet fit once insulation and equipment take space. Check whether packaging, pallets, restraints and any partitions allow the proposed loading pattern. Ask for photographs or drawings that show cleaning access, drainage where specified and door-seal construction. Do not automatically choose the biggest box: extra volume is only useful when the chassis, cooling arrangement and actual cargo plan can support it. [1][2]

## Compare refrigeration output under matching conditions

A refrigeration unit’s input power is not the same as its cooling capacity. Even a published cooling-capacity figure needs its ambient temperature, return-air temperature and operating mode. Carrier’s Supra S10 specification, for example, presents separate outputs for diesel operation and electric standby under stated conditions. It is an example of how to read a rating, not a recommendation that this unit suits every Chinese truck or route. Request the proposed unit’s own current selection calculation for the specified body, goods and duty. Reject comparisons in which one supplier quotes a warmer test condition while another quotes the demanding condition the buyer actually needs. [3]

## Match the power arrangement to stops and parking

Truck Home explains the difference between traditional engine-driven and independently powered refrigeration equipment. That is a useful starting point, but body length alone should not decide the system. Modern electric and standby arrangements require their own equipment-specific documentation. Consider what happens during a delivery stop, at a loading dock, overnight and during a power interruption. If electric standby is included, specify the available supply and ask the manufacturer or installer to confirm compatibility. Carrier publishes separate installation information for different unit families and options; a generic brochure cannot establish mounting, wiring or standby suitability for an exact installation. These are questions for the qualified supplier, not instructions to improvise a conversion. [1][4]

## Make the chassis–body interface somebody’s responsibility

Request approved installation drawings and identify the party responsible for the complete vehicle’s integration. Check the proposed mounting, electrical supply, compressor drive or other interface with the relevant chassis and unit documentation. FUSO’s European body-options material illustrates why vehicle makers provide bodybuilder information and dedicated interfaces; it does not certify a different brand’s conversion. In an export purchase, the bodybuilder, chassis seller and refrigeration supplier may be different businesses. The contract should say who resolves an integration fault and what evidence each party supplies. A supplier saying that every component is a famous brand is not a substitute for responsibility for how those components work together. [6]

## Agree on a meaningful acceptance demonstration

A brief video of an empty box reaching a setpoint is not the whole delivery test. Agree in advance on the equipment identity, ambient conditions, pre-cooling procedure, measurement locations, instrument records and operating mode. Ask how the demonstration will reflect the agreed loading arrangement and door-opening pattern, and how temperature recovery will be assessed. There is no single recovery time or threshold appropriate to all products and routes, so put the acceptance criteria in the order after competent confirmation. Preserve the records and record any differences between the demonstrated vehicle and the one shipped. A test with clear boundaries is more useful than an impressive-looking display with no conditions attached.

## Compare the complete quote and the local repair plan

Use the same brief for every supplier. Compare chassis identity, completed mass and payload evidence, usable body layout, refrigeration model, standby equipment, monitoring, installation, acceptance records and documents. Ask which items are included and which remain the buyer’s responsibility. Separately name the destination workshop, parts route and contact for refrigeration faults; chassis service alone does not establish support for the body or unit. Obtain separate warranty terms for each part and identify who coordinates a claim involving more than one supplier. This guide does not estimate freight, duty, running cost or promised delivery time. Those figures need an actual route-specific commercial offer.

## The best choice is the one that proves the duty

For frequent urban drops, door access, stop pattern and cooling recovery deserve particular attention. For longer runs, sustained operation, energy provision and service availability become central. Neither pattern automatically requires a particular brand, body length or refrigeration type. Prefer the offer that answers the full operating brief with traceable documents and an agreed demonstration. Pause the order if cargo capacity is described only by a base-chassis number, cooling is quoted without conditions, or nobody owns the integration problem. Before shipment, verify destination-specific vehicle approval and cargo-compliance requirements with the competent local parties; this procurement guide is not an import permit or a certification of the completed truck.

## FAQ

**Is a larger insulated box always the better purchase?**

No. Usable volume must be assessed alongside completed-vehicle payload, loading layout and the refrigeration arrangement. Space that prevents airflow or exceeds a permitted load is not useful capacity.

**Does a lower thermostat setting prove stronger cooling?**

No. Setpoint and cooling capacity are different. Compare the proposed unit’s rated output and selection evidence at the conditions relevant to the actual body and route.

**What should be checked before accepting the truck?**

The completed vehicle’s identity and load evidence, body layout, installed unit, power interfaces and agreed temperature demonstration should match the order. Retain the records and resolve material differences before shipment.
