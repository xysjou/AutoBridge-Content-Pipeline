# Buying a Used Car with a Replaced Windscreen: ADAS Evidence

A replacement windscreen can leave a used-car buyer with two different questions. Is the glass installation satisfactory, and has any driver-assistance work connected with that replacement been completed? A receipt reading “windscreen fitted” may answer neither in sufficient detail. The second question deserves its own evidence, especially when the car will leave the seller's country before the buyer can arrange a familiar workshop inspection.

The purchasing decision should follow the repair event through to its documented outcome. That means identifying the fitted systems, establishing the relevant vehicle manufacturer's procedure and obtaining the result of any required work. It does not mean assuming every car needs the same calibration, or treating a short drive without a warning message as proof that a camera has been correctly adjusted.

## Identify what was changed and what is fitted

Begin with the repair invoice and the vehicle's identity. Ask when the glass was replaced, which part was fitted and whether any camera, bracket or related component was removed or replaced. Request the repairer's description rather than drawing conclusions solely from an exterior photograph. A visible housing behind the mirror is a reason to identify the equipment, not enough to name its exact functions or calibration method.

I-CAR's explanation of its OEM Calibration Requirements Search shows why system identity matters. Its entries distinguish sensor locations and system configurations; a rear-view-only camera and one used within a surround-view system can have different requirements. The same discipline applies to the front of the car: use the actual model, year and equipment rather than instructions found for a vaguely similar vehicle.

For a remote purchase, ask the workshop to connect the repair record to the offered vehicle identification number. If the seller provides an undated sample report, treat it as an example of the workshop's paperwork, not the car's completed work. This simple distinction prevents a persuasive-looking document from becoming evidence for a vehicle it never examined.

## Establish the procedure before interpreting the result

I-CAR's windscreen article is explicit about the limits of its search tool. It helps identify calibration events and necessary resources, but does not supply the repair procedures or replace the manufacturer's information. Its displayed model coverage is also bounded. A vehicle absent from that tool is not thereby exempt from calibration; obtain the relevant OEM information through an appropriately equipped repair provider.

The buyer does not need to reproduce a technical repair manual in the purchase contract. A useful request is narrower: ask the repairer to identify the applicable procedure and explain whether the recorded repair triggers further work. If the answer is that calibration was not required, request the vehicle-specific basis for that conclusion. A statement that the workshop “normally does not do it” does not resolve the case.

Likewise, wording such as “not identified” should not be read as “not required.” I-CAR explains that the former can mean the service information did not specify an item. Preserve that distinction in translations of a report. Converting uncertainty into a reassuring yes-or-no answer makes the document easier to read but less useful for making a safe purchase.

## Separate a scan, a calibration and a completed repair

A diagnostic scan and a calibration are different pieces of evidence. Ask what the supplied report actually records. Does it show fault-code information, the completion of an adjustment routine, or the outcome of the full prescribed repair? Do not let a generic “system checked” line substitute for whichever operation the manufacturer's procedure calls for.

Bosch's glass-fitters brochure describes static and dynamic calibration, including systems that use a combination. It is a 2019 equipment document, useful for explaining why one workshop method cannot be presumed universal. It is not a repair instruction for a newly offered vehicle. Bosch's current UK and Ireland equipment overview also lists different targets and equipment coverage, reinforcing a practical purchasing question: can the proposed workshop support this exact vehicle and system?

Ask the technician to determine the method; do not improvise a calibration drive based on an online checklist. Nor should a prospective buyer deliberately provoke emergency braking or lane intervention to demonstrate that the system works. Any required verification belongs within a competent provider's safe, manufacturer-directed process, with an intelligible record of the outcome.

## Read a result that can be traced

Thatcham Research's May 2019 ADAS repair position calls for auditable records, competent technicians and evidence of operation within the manufacturer's tolerances. This is an industry repair position, not a worldwide statute. For buyers, its value is the emphasis on traceability: a calibration result should belong to an identifiable vehicle and an identifiable piece of work.

A sensible purchasing record includes the repairer, vehicle identity, date, affected system, relevant procedure and completed result. If another business performed the calibration, ask for that business's record rather than relying only on the glass company's invoice. Link the dates so that the result clearly follows the repair it is supposed to close. An earlier calibration does not establish that later glass work was addressed.

Retain the original-language report alongside a translation when buying across borders. Translate the result and any qualifications faithfully, including incomplete or unsuccessful steps. An interpreter should not upgrade “appointment booked” into “calibration passed,” or omit a limitation because it sounds technical. Ask the workshop to clarify an ambiguous result before accepting an editorial rewrite of it.

## Treat unfinished calibration as unfinished work

ICBC's British Columbia glass-claims policy separates successful calibration from cases where the work could not be performed. It describes documenting the reason and informing the customer when required calibration remains outstanding. Those insurer-program rules do not govern every international purchase, but the distinction is useful: a repair can have a genuine explanation for delay without being complete.

For an overseas transaction, identify who will resolve any outstanding step and where. A seller's promise that a destination dealer can do it should be checked with that dealer for the exact vehicle. Parts, technical access and appointments are practical questions, not assumptions to settle after shipment. Avoid inserting an estimated price into the deal unless a real provider has quoted the actual scope.

Before collection, reconcile the final glass record and calibration evidence. If the two cannot be connected, keep the issue open. If the provider concludes further investigation is needed, do not relabel the car as fully checked merely because there are no visible dashboard warnings. The useful outcome is a documented repair status, including any remaining limitation, rather than a blanket claim that all driver assistance is guaranteed.

## Questions buyers ask

**Does every replacement windscreen require the same calibration?** No. Identify the fitted equipment and consult the applicable manufacturer procedure. Static, dynamic and combined processes are not interchangeable purchasing choices.

**Is a fault-code printout sufficient?** It depends on what the procedure requires and what the printout actually establishes. Ask separately for the calibration outcome when calibration is required.

**What if the repairer cannot support the model?** Obtain a qualified alternative's vehicle-specific plan before treating the repair as closed. A workshop's equipment brand alone does not establish coverage.

**What should accompany the sale?** Keep the glass repair documentation, the procedure basis and the actual completed result together. This focused file complements broader [vehicle-purchasing guides](https://www.autobridgeexport.com/guides/); it does not replace a complete used-car inspection.
