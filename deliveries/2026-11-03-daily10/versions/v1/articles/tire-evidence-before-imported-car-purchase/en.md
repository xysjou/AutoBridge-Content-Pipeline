# Tire Evidence to Request Before Buying an Imported Car

A photograph of four clean wheels tells an overseas buyer very little about the tires being purchased. The sidewall lettering may be unreadable, the inner shoulder may be out of view, and the spare may not appear at all. For an imported passenger car, the useful question is not whether the tires look new. It is whether the fitted set matches the vehicle and whether its condition has been assessed well enough to support the delivery decision.

This guide recommends a tire-by-tire evidence record, rather than a seller's single description such as “good tires.” It separates specification, age, measured condition and unresolved damage. That distinction matters when a buyer cannot inspect the car in person: a correct size does not establish condition, and generous tread does not establish correct fitment. The following method is a purchasing framework, not a remote roadworthiness certificate or a substitute for a qualified tire professional.

## Start with the car's specification, not a replacement listing

Ask for a legible photograph of the vehicle's tire-information label and the applicable owner's-manual specification. NHTSA directs owners to those references for the correct tire size and recommended inflation. Its guidance also distinguishes the pressure recommended for the vehicle from the maximum marked on the tire sidewall. Copying the sidewall maximum into a handover sheet would answer the wrong question.

Record the exact vehicle variant alongside that evidence. If the wheels have been changed, request the previous specification and the basis for the replacement fitment. “The wheel fits” is not a complete explanation. Continental's December 2006 plus-sizing bulletin, explicitly scoped to the United States, Canada and Mexico, illustrates the engineering questions: load capacity, speed rating, rolling circumference, compatible rim dimensions and clearance. It is a historical technical reference, not a current worldwide approval rule. Ask a qualified specialist to resolve a non-original setup against the actual car's requirements.

Do not use an online marketplace's list of compatible models as the only support. A buyer can instead ask the seller to identify which vehicle specification was checked, who checked it and which installed markings were compared. If the response contains only a brand name and wheel diameter, the fitment review is still incomplete.

## Give every tire its own identity

Create separate entries for front left, front right, rear left, rear right and any spare. For each, request the tire manufacturer, product line, complete size marking and service description. Bridgestone's sidewall guide explains why the lettering contains more than wheel diameter: it describes tire type, dimensions and performance-related ratings. The owner's manual remains the starting point for deciding what belongs on the car.

Use photographs that connect each close-up to its wheel position. Five excellent close-ups without positions can still leave the buyer unsure which tire was inspected. Ask the inspector to retain the original files and date the record. This is particularly helpful if the seller later replaces one tire before collection: the replacement should receive a new entry rather than silently inheriting the old photograph and result.

A mixed set calls for an explanation, not an automatic diagnosis. Different front and rear specifications can be intentional on some cars. Equally, the presence of different markings should not be dismissed just because the vehicle can be driven. Have the actual combination assessed against manufacturer guidance; avoid a blanket instruction that every car needs four identical sizes.

## Read age alongside condition

Michelin's tire-replacement guidance states that tire life depends on design, use, environment and maintenance, rather than one mileage number. For a tire carrying a DOT date code, the final four digits identify its manufacturing week and year. Record the marking as seen before interpreting it. A registration date or seller's installation invoice is not the same thing as a tire's manufacturing date.

The date helps frame the inspection but cannot complete it. Michelin recommends professional inspection and following the vehicle manufacturer's replacement guidance for original-equipment tires. Its age recommendations should be identified as manufacturer guidance, not presented as a universal statutory limit for every destination. Nor should a buyer treat a recently manufactured tire as proven undamaged.

Request a professional assessment if markings cannot be read or a tire's history is uncertain. “Date not visible in supplied evidence” is a useful, honest entry. Guessing a date from an indistinct image is not. Where a clearer view requires access to the other side of a tire, let a properly equipped professional obtain it safely.

## Ask for measurements and observations separately

A useful condition report distinguishes measured tread depth from visual observations. Ask where readings were taken and which wheel each reading belongs to. One attractive number for the entire vehicle hides differences between shoulders and between tires. Do not set an arbitrary worldwide acceptance threshold from this guide: the buyer's intended use, applicable local requirements and specialist advice need their own consideration.

Bridgestone's safety guidance identifies uneven wear, cracked sidewalls, bulges and unusual vibration as reasons for attention. These observations do not all mean the same repair. For example, an uneven wear pattern deserves assessment rather than a remote claim that alignment alone will fix it. Ask the inspector to describe the finding and recommended action without turning a photograph into an unsupported mechanical diagnosis.

Include pressure readings with the measurement conditions, not merely “pressures checked.” NHTSA's summer guidance recommends checking cold tires and checking the spare, while warning against using the tire's maximum pressure as the vehicle target. For procurement, the important record connects the measured value, the correct vehicle reference and any adjustment performed. It should not imply that inflating a visibly damaged tire makes it acceptable.

## Resolve exceptions before calling the set accepted

The seller and buyer should agree how unresolved tire findings affect collection. A practical record can distinguish an accepted item, an item awaiting professional assessment and an item agreed for replacement. These are commercial tracking categories recommended here, not legal grades. A replacement promise is separate from evidence that replacement has happened.

If replacement is agreed, obtain the final fitted specification, invoice or service record, updated photographs and any relevant professional confirmation. Preserve both versions of the record so the reason for the change remains visible. Do not accept a document describing a different set simply because its date is recent. The point is to identify the tires that will actually accompany the purchased vehicle.

For a car with no conventional spare, record its documented mobility provision rather than assuming something is missing. If a temporary spare or repair kit is supplied, ask for its identification and condition to be checked under the applicable instructions. ## Questions buyers ask

**Can a tire photograph prove that the car is safe to drive?** No. It can document visible markings and some damage, but the acceptance decision may require measurements and professional examination. Do not substitute remote image review for an on-site assessment.

**Does substantial tread mean an older tire is acceptable?** Not by itself. Age, damage, wear and the manufacturer's advice all matter. Record the evidence instead of reducing the decision to appearance or a single date.

**Should the seller replace every mismatched tire?** Establish the intended fitment first. Ask a qualified professional to assess the actual combination, including any original staggered arrangement, before agreeing the remedy.

**What is the most useful final deliverable?** A dated, wheel-positioned record of the installed tires, the vehicle specification used, measurements, observations and resolved or outstanding actions. That lets both parties discuss the same physical set rather than a vague description.
