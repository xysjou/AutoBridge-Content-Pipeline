# Petrol compatibility: check octane and ethanol separately

Two petrol pumps can display similar-looking numbers without describing the same fuel requirement. A number attached to an octane scale answers a different question from an E-number describing ethanol content. For an imported petrol vehicle, compatibility therefore needs two separate comparisons: the engine's octane requirement against the destination fuel's octane specification, and the manufacturer's ethanol allowance against that fuel's blend description.

A familiar grade name such as “premium” does not complete either comparison. Neither does a seller's statement that the car is modern or that it carries an emissions badge. Ask what the exact engine's applicable owner's manual permits, then ask what the fuel supplier actually sells where the vehicle will be used. The purpose is to decide whether the intended car and available fuel match, not to choose the largest number on a pump.

## Octane and ethanol are separate questions

US government consumer guidance explains octane in terms of resistance to engine knock. Ethanol labelling describes a different property. The UK government's explanation of E5 and E10 explicitly distinguishes the ethanol label from octane. Reading both markings matters because agreement on one does not establish agreement on the other.

Record the rating system alongside an octane number. RON and AKI are not interchangeable labels for a bare number. Where a manual provides a value expressed as (R+M)/2 and another expressed as RON, keep each attached to its own notation. Do not create a universal conversion rule or assume a fixed adjustment makes any fuel label equivalent to any manual requirement.

For ethanol, retain the complete blend designation and the manufacturer's wording. The US Department of Energy describes E15 as petrol containing 10.5–15 percent ethanol. That source-specific description is not a reason to infer that every E-number is an exact measured concentration in every location. Obtain the supplier's specification rather than guessing from a familiar nickname.

## Find the manual for the engine, not just the badge

Before interpreting a fuel paragraph, establish which vehicle and engine it covers. Ask the seller for the original sales market, engine identification, relevant model year and the applicable manual edition. These are recommended purchasing checks, not a claim that a regulator universally demands this list.

A manual for another market or engine may be useful as an example of how requirements are presented, but it does not establish compatibility for your purchase. The Hyundai manual linked below is identified as DN8, 2027, en_US. Its fuel section shows octane notations, an ethanol limit and restrictions concerning methanol and E85. It is an example of reading several requirements together, not a global Hyundai fuel rule or proof of a retail model identity beyond that document.

Keep a copy of the relevant fuel paragraph with the vehicle details. If a seller supplies a cropped screenshot without the manual's identity, request the complete reference. The aim is not to accumulate paperwork; it is to avoid approving fuel on the basis of instructions written for a different engine.

## Required and recommended are not the same instruction

US government octane guidance distinguishes a manufacturer's required grade from a recommended grade. Preserve that distinction when taking notes or translating a manual. Do not change “recommended” into “required,” and do not weaken a requirement into an optional preference.

If the seller says premium fuel is always better, ask for the applicable manufacturer statement. This guide makes no universal claim that a higher grade improves performance, economy or ownership costs. The practical question is what the manual requires or recommends for the engine being sourced, and whether a local product meets that wording.

Also avoid answering an octane question with an ethanol answer. A statement that a vehicle accepts a particular ethanol blend does not identify its octane requirement. Conversely, a suitable octane label does not settle whether the ethanol content is permitted. Both entries should remain visible in the purchase decision.

## National guidance is not worldwide vehicle approval

The US E15 guidance describes a US authorization context. It should not be carried across to a Chinese-market vehicle, another market's engine or every imported petrol car as proof of manufacturer compatibility. Regulatory permission within one jurisdiction and manufacturer guidance for a specific engine are different kinds of evidence.

Similarly, the UK compatibility checker is a UK-market resource. A matching name can help direct an inquiry, but it should not automatically approve a differently specified vehicle sold elsewhere. Keep the market and engine associated with a result, and ask the manufacturer when the match is uncertain.

US government consumer information identifies E85 as fuel for flex-fuel vehicles. Do not treat the ability to use another ethanol blend as evidence of E85 compatibility. The manufacturer's applicable instructions remain the place to resolve what the actual vehicle permits.

## A missing database entry is an unanswered question

The New South Wales government's E10 information explains that its compatibility database relies on manufacturer information. A missing vehicle can reflect absent data or a newer model rather than established incompatibility. Therefore “not found” should not be rewritten as “incompatible.” It also should not become “probably compatible.”

Save the result and identify what is missing: the engine, model year, market or manufacturer statement. Request clarification from the appropriate manufacturer channel. If the only answer is that a similar vehicle uses the fuel, keep the purchase question open until the actual vehicle is covered.

This approach avoids both unnecessary rejection and unsupported reassurance. A database can be a useful starting point without being a complete catalogue of every version sold in every country.

## Ask the fuel supplier a precise question

Obtain the specification for the product you expect to buy at the destination. Record its octane value and scale, ethanol blend information, supplier and the date of the information. A photograph can help identify the pump, but a marketing name alone does not explain the fuel's complete compatibility with your engine.

Compare that information with the manual rather than with a seller's memory of a similarly named grade. If the supplier describes several products under related names, identify the specific one. If regular access to that product is central to your decision, establish its availability along your intended routes instead of assuming a national brand name guarantees every location stocks it.

If either octane or ethanol remains unresolved, a sensible commercial response is to pause the fuel-dependent purchase decision. That is editorial purchasing advice, not a government prohibition on buying the vehicle.

## A compact record that keeps both answers visible

Use two columns in your own comparison: “Vehicle requirement” and “Destination fuel.” Under octane, record the manual wording, number and rating system opposite the supplier's matching specification. Under ethanol, record the permitted blend or limit opposite the product's ethanol description. Attach the manual identity and supplier reference to the same record.

Give each comparison its own conclusion. “Octane matched; ethanol awaiting manufacturer confirmation” is more useful than a single unsupported “fuel compatible.” Release the decision only when the remaining uncertainty is answered to your satisfaction with applicable evidence. If the car, engine, destination or fuel product changes, revisit the affected comparison rather than carrying the earlier answer forward unchanged.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I convert any AKI number to RON using a fixed addition?

This guide does not provide a universal conversion. Keep the stated rating system and obtain manufacturer or supplier information that lets you compare the applicable requirements directly.

### Does E10 compatibility mean premium petrol is unnecessary?

These describe different questions. Ethanol compatibility does not determine the required or recommended octane grade. Read both parts of the manual.

### Does US E15 authorization approve my imported vehicle?

Not by itself. US authorization is jurisdiction-specific and does not replace manufacturer evidence for the actual engine and market.

### What if the checker does not list my car?

Treat the result as unresolved. The NSW guidance explains why missing information can cause omissions; seek the applicable manufacturer answer rather than assuming either compatibility or incompatibility.
