# Puncture-mobility equipment: what should actually arrive with the car?

A tidy case beneath the boot floor can create false reassurance. The compressor may be present, the bottle may look unopened, and the moulded tray may look complete, yet the sealant can already be beyond its expiry date. A buyer who discovers that only after a puncture has bought the appearance of preparedness rather than a dependable plan.

The handover question is not whether every car has a spare wheel. Different vehicles use different arrangements, and a spare is not universally supplied. It is whether the equipment promised for this particular car is complete, identifiable, within its stated service life and stored as intended. This guide concerns the supplied tools and consumables. It is not a tyre-condition assessment or roadside repair tutorial.

## Identify the intended arrangement before counting pieces

Ask the seller which mobility provision belongs to the actual vehicle: a repair kit, an appropriate spare with its associated equipment, or another documented arrangement. Compare that answer with the relevant owner’s manual and the agreed sale description. An empty recess does not prove a spare is missing; a wheel that fits into the boot does not prove it belongs on the car.

For a repair kit, identify the kit maker and product reference as well as the vehicle application. For a spare, seek the compatibility information for the exact vehicle, not merely a statement that it came from a similar model. The RAC’s space-saver guidance is a useful reminder that temporary spares are not universally interchangeable and need suitable storage. These are product questions, not grounds for inventing a rule that all imported cars need the same equipment.

If the seller has substituted an aftermarket kit, ask for its instructions and evidence that it is appropriate for the proposed use. A replacement is not automatically unsuitable, but a familiar brand or a generic product description does not answer compatibility. Clarify what is being supplied before treating the car as ready for handover.

## Look at the contents without consuming them

Arrange an inspection of the storage area and all included components. Use the correct manual’s inventory rather than a universal shopping list. Depending on the specified system, the relevant pieces may include a compressor, sealant container, hose, power lead or other accessories. Presence, identification and condition are separate observations: a component can be present but unidentifiable, damaged or mismatched.

Ask for clear photographs of the complete contents together, the product labels and the normal storage position. For a remote purchase, photographs make missing pieces easier to discuss, but they do not certify electrical operation or suitability. Avoid asking someone to discharge sealant merely to prove the bottle is there. If functional checking is needed, let a qualified provider follow the manufacturer’s approved procedure without unnecessary deployment.

Also check whether the instructions are readable and accessible to the person who will use the car. A sealed bag with no intelligible product information is not a complete explanation of the system. The buyer should know where the instructions are, what product they refer to and where to seek assistance if the situation falls outside those instructions.

## The bottle needs its own decision

The British Tyre Manufacturers’ Association explains that mobility sealants have a finite life and that kit instructions matter. Toyota’s official explainer and its replies also direct users to check expiry and replace consumables after use. Neither an unopened appearance nor a low vehicle mileage establishes that the sealant is still usable.

Read the actual expiry marking and distinguish it from a manufacturing date or an unreadable code. If the date cannot be interpreted confidently, request clarification from the product supplier. For a vehicle awaiting delivery, agree what remaining shelf life will be acceptable when it arrives, rather than checking only the date of the sales photograph. This is a recommended commercial agreement, not a universal manufacturer threshold.

A used, expired or unidentified container should be a named unresolved item, with an agreed replacement route. Ask whether the replacement is compatible with the rest of the kit and whether it can be sourced in the destination market. A promise to send “some sealant” later is less useful than an identified product, responsible supplier and agreed delivery arrangement.

## Limits belong to the exact product

Continental describes its ContiMobilityKit as a temporary system combining a compressor and sealant, with its own limitations and shelf life. The cited Mazda CX-30 manual edition gives its own contents and exclusion cases, including expired sealant and multiple punctures. These examples show why a buyer should obtain the relevant instructions; they do not establish universal limits for every kit.

Do not transfer a permitted puncture size, driving distance or speed from one manufacturer’s page to another product. The same caution applies to exclusions. A seller’s reassurance that a kit “handles punctures” does not identify which circumstances it covers or when assistance is needed. Ask for the limitations that apply to the exact supplied system and keep them with the car.

Michelin’s repair guidance makes the distinction between temporary mobility and a proper repair important: professional examination is needed to determine repairability. A sealant bottle is not a promise of permanent repair. BTMA also warns that aftermarket sealants can affect valves, tyre-pressure monitoring systems and repairability. Choose replacements through appropriate compatibility advice rather than assuming every bottle with a similar nozzle is equivalent.

## Storage is part of completeness

Ask the seller to demonstrate where the equipment is normally retained and how it is secured according to the vehicle or product instructions. A loose wheel, loose tools or a kit placed casually among luggage is not the same as a correctly stored arrangement. For a spare-wheel provision, establish that the associated equipment and retention arrangement are included as agreed, not just the wheel itself.

Consider access as well as location. If the storage compartment sits beneath the luggage, familiarise yourself with the arrangement before packing for a journey. This is not a recommendation to unpack or attempt repairs in an unsafe roadside position. If there is an incident, prioritise personal safety and follow the vehicle instructions and appropriate assistance advice.

Missing retention pieces deserve the same attention as missing tools. Ask how replacements will be obtained and avoid accepting an improvised storage solution merely because the boot lid closes. The seller’s demonstration should show the agreed arrangement, without presenting photographs as proof of roadworthiness.

## Turn a vague promise into a clear handover agreement

A useful handover inventory names the supplied system, lists the pieces identified in its instructions, records the sealant expiry where applicable and describes the storage arrangement. Add any replacement that remains outstanding, who will supply it and when it is expected. Keep the agreement alongside the sale documents so a later dispute does not depend on whether “kit included” meant a complete serviceable system or an empty case.

If a minor missing item has an identified compatible replacement, buyer and seller can agree how to resolve it. If the kit cannot be identified, the sealant status is unclear or a spare’s compatibility is unresolved, consider postponing acceptance of that part of the handover until the evidence is available. These are purchasing recommendations, not legal inspection standards or a certification of the car.

## Questions at handover

### Does every car need a spare wheel?

No universal assumption is appropriate. Establish the arrangement specified for the vehicle and promised in the sale, then check that arrangement’s completeness and suitability.

### Is an unopened sealant bottle enough?

No. Read the expiry and product identification, confirm compatibility, and establish whether replacement is needed. Appearance alone does not establish remaining service life.

### Can the seller prove the kit works by using the sealant?

Unnecessary deployment can consume the product. Seek manufacturer-approved checking by a suitable provider where needed; inventory photographs alone are not a functional test.

### Is a temporary kit a permanent repair?

No. Michelin’s guidance distinguishes temporary mobility from a repairability decision made after professional examination. Follow the supplied product’s instructions and seek appropriate assistance rather than relying on a generic distance or speed limit.
