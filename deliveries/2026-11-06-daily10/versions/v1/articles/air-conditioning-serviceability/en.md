# Buying a car abroad: check whether its air conditioning can be serviced at home

A seller sends a video: the dashboard is lit, the fan is running and someone says the air is cold. It may be a useful demonstration of operation at that moment. It does not identify the refrigerant, establish which compressor oil belongs in the system or show that a workshop at your destination can service the car correctly. For an overseas purchase, those unanswered questions can matter as much as the demonstration itself.

The buying decision is therefore broader than “does the air conditioning work?” Ask whether the exact system can be identified, whether its service history makes sense and whether an appropriate local workshop will accept it. This is a procurement guide, not a charging procedure. Refrigerant handling, investigation of unknown contents and compressor work belong with qualified professionals using the applicable vehicle information.

## Start with the car's label, not a model-name search

Request a readable photograph of the actual vehicle's refrigerant label, together with enough surrounding context to connect it to that car. Ask for the relevant air-conditioning service records as well. A generic brochure image, a photograph from another vehicle or an answer based only on the model name is a weaker basis for planning service.

Volvo's Malta support page for the S60 provides a useful illustration of the issue. In that specific vehicle and market documentation, refrigerant specification can involve R134a or R1234yf depending on market, and the label under the bonnet identifies the fitted equipment. This is not a statement that every imported vehicle uses either of those refrigerants. It shows why a market-specific document and the actual label are more useful than assuming one specification follows a familiar model name everywhere.

Have the proposed workshop compare the label with manufacturer information applicable to the exact vehicle. The buyer's job is to obtain the evidence and arrange a competent interpretation, not to infer a service instruction from a label fragment. If a label is missing, damaged or inconsistent with the records, treat the identity as unresolved until a qualified professional has investigated it.

## Refrigerant identity does not answer the oil question

Compressor oil is not a generic extra that can be settled with “use the usual air-conditioning oil.” DENSO's technical guidance explains that correct formulation matters and that oils and refrigerants are not freely interchangeable. Its product information describes different oils for different compressors and refrigerants.

That distinction becomes especially important when a seller or workshop reduces the answer to “it is an electric car.” DENSO identifies ND-oil 11 for its electric scroll compressors. That product-specific statement is not a rule for all electric vehicles. MAHLE's filling-information guidance also notes that some electric compressors use special PAG oils. Together, these sources show why a blanket instruction that every electric compressor takes POE is unsound.

Ask the workshop to establish the compressor and applicable manufacturer oil specification from appropriate service information. Do not choose oil from propulsion type alone, and do not substitute a convenient product because its container says it is suitable for air conditioning. This article gives no oil quantity, charging quantity or substitution recipe. A correct purchasing question should lead to a professional service answer, not encourage the buyer to perform the work.

## Ask a workshop a specific question before ordering

“Can you repair imported cars?” is too broad. Send the workshop the actual vehicle information, label photograph and relevant service records. Ask whether it can support that air-conditioning system, obtain the necessary technical information and use the appropriate equipment and specified materials. For a vehicle with an electric compressor, include that detail rather than letting it disappear inside a general booking request.

A useful reply identifies what the workshop can confirm now and what it can only determine after inspecting the car. It should also explain whether the work would be performed there or referred elsewhere. A referral is not automatically a problem; an unidentified eventual repairer is an unresolved part of the purchase.

Avoid treating a broad verbal “yes” as confirmation that every future fault can be repaired immediately. No workshop reply can guarantee all future parts availability or repair times. What you can reasonably seek is a credible service route for the known system, an explanation of remaining uncertainty and a clear contact responsible for the next assessment.

If the car is still abroad, it may be practical to arrange a qualified inspection there before committing. Agree who receives the report and what questions it should answer. The point is to resolve identity and condition, not merely to obtain a second video showing cold air.

## Read service history for changes, not just stamps

Ask whether refrigerant, compressor or related service-fluid specifications have changed during previous repairs. Where a record mentions a replacement or conversion, request the supporting documentation and let a competent workshop assess what it means for the vehicle as it now exists. An original label can be informative without proving that an altered system still contains its original materials.

Be particularly careful with an unexplained promise that a cheaper gas works just as well. The US Environmental Protection Agency discusses fire and explosion risks associated with hydrocarbon refrigerant substitutions in motor-vehicle air conditioning. That warning should not be simplified into a claim that every flammable refrigerant is the same, or that every refrigerant carrying a hazard classification is an illicit substitute.

The EPA's acceptable-refrigerant information also distinguishes refrigerants and their conditions of use within its US remit. It does not establish a worldwide retrofit permission or prohibition. If a seller proposes a conversion, have the appropriate destination professional establish both technical suitability and applicable local requirements. A proposal to “sort the gas out after arrival” is not an adequate explanation of an unknown system.

## When to proceed, and when to pause

A reasonable basis to continue is a legible label tied to the offered vehicle, service information that supports the identified configuration, and a destination workshop that has assessed whether it can support that configuration. Keep any remaining inspection conditions visible in the commercial decision. These are recommended purchasing safeguards, not a universal legal document list.

Pause when the label cannot be reconciled with the records, when an undocumented substitution is suspected, or when no qualified service provider can explain how it would assess the system. A seller's willingness to produce cold air does not resolve any of those gaps. Nor does a discount identify what is inside the circuit.

If a professional inspection resolves the discrepancy, reconsider the purchase using that new information. If uncertainty remains, weigh it as a real ownership constraint rather than assuming a future workshop will discover an easy solution. The most useful outcome is not a folder of generic reassurance; it is a clear answer about the offered car and the place where you intend to maintain it.

## Questions buyers ask

### Is a cold-air video worthless?

No. It may show the system operating at that moment. It does not prove refrigerant identity, oil compatibility, repair history or destination service capability.

### Does every electric compressor use POE oil?

No universal rule is supported. DENSO's ND-oil 11 application is product-specific, while MAHLE discusses some electric compressors using special PAG. The exact manufacturer specification is the relevant starting point.

### Can I have a missing label replaced and proceed?

A replacement label alone does not establish the actual contents or explain alterations. Ask a qualified professional to resolve the system's identity and supporting documentation before relying on it.

### Does US refrigerant guidance apply to my destination?

Not automatically. EPA material is US-specific. Technical concerns can inform your questions, but destination requirements and any proposed conversion need their own applicable assessment.

### Should I ask the seller to top it up before shipping?

Do not use a top-up as a substitute for diagnosis or identification. Request professional assessment and documented findings rather than prescribing a repair from a distance.
