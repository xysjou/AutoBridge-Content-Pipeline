# Airbag Repair Evidence: What Used-Car Buyers Should Request

A repaired dashboard can look convincing while leaving an overseas buyer with unanswered questions about the restraint system behind it. The relevant purchasing question is not simply whether the airbag warning is off. It is whether previous deployment or repair has been disclosed, replacement parts are traceable, and a qualified person has assessed the exact vehicle.

This is a document-and-inspection guide for used-car procurement, not an airbag repair manual. Do not remove covers, probe wiring or dismantle modules to satisfy a seller's evidence request. Ask a qualified workshop to assess the system using the information applicable to that vehicle.

## Begin with the event that made repair necessary

Ask whether any airbags deployed, whether restraint components were replaced for another reason, and whether the vehicle was previously stolen or involved in a collision. Request available damage photographs, the insurer or auction description, the repair estimate and the completed invoice. An estimate describes intended work; it is not evidence that all of that work happened.

The US [NHTSA replacement-inflator warning](https://www.nhtsa.gov/air-bags/deadly-air-bag-replacements) directs buyers with relevant deployment, crash, theft or repair history toward qualified inspection. It also warns against inspecting airbag assemblies yourself. That is the appropriate direction for a procurement request: establish the history, then commission competent assessment rather than relying on seller demonstrations.

Keep the event account linked to the VIN. If the seller sends photographs of several cars, identify which images belong to the offered vehicle before drawing conclusions. A repair file for a similar model or another unit in a fleet does not resolve the offered car's condition.

## Separate three kinds of evidence

First is **history evidence**: what is known about the event and the condition before repair. Second is **parts and work evidence**: what was supplied and fitted, by whom, and for which vehicle. Third is **inspection evidence**: what a qualified assessor observed or checked after the work.

These records should connect rather than substitute for one another. A parts invoice does not prove that the part was installed in this vehicle. A photograph of an intact steering wheel does not identify the component behind it. A diagnostic report does not, by itself, explain the origin of an undocumented replacement part.

[NHTSA's general airbag guidance](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle-safety/air-bags) explains that airbags supplement seat belts and can deploy only once. Consequently, a purchasing brief concerned with deployment should not focus solely on replacing an interior cover. Ask the workshop to define the applicable restraint-system repair scope, rather than creating a universal replacement list yourself.

| Evidence layer | Useful request | Unresolved question if missing |
|---|---|---|
| Event history | Dated damage images and deployment account | What prompted the repair? |
| Repair scope | Itemized completed work tied to VIN | Which restraint-related items were addressed? |
| Part provenance | Supplier invoice and identifiable part details | Where did the replacement come from? |
| Workshop basis | Applicable manufacturer repair information | Was the assessment appropriate to this vehicle? |
| Completion assessment | Signed findings, date and stated limitations | What was actually checked afterward? |
| Handover condition | Current warning status and unresolved concerns | Has anything changed since the assessment? |

The table is an editorial purchasing checklist. Its documents are recommended evidence, not a worldwide legal certificate package.

## Treat the warning lamp as a signal with model-specific meaning

A seller may offer a video showing the warning lamp going out. Preserve it as an observation, but do not convert it into proof of parts authenticity or complete repair. Ask whether the observed sequence matches the vehicle's own manual.

For example, [Ford Australia's warning-lamp guidance](https://www.ford.com.au/owners/vehicle-support/indicator-icons/airbag-warning-lamp/) identifies failure to illuminate at starting, continued flashing or remaining on as malfunction indications and advises an authorized-dealer check. That is Ford guidance in its stated market, not a universal timing sequence for every imported vehicle.

The [European online Model 3 manual](https://www.tesla.com/ownersmanual/model3/en_eu/GUID-533FEEF4-F3D7-4D29-B3EE-378A72152255.html) says a persistent airbag warning calls for immediate service and no driving. Apply that instruction to its actual vehicle scope. For another model or production version, obtain the corresponding manual rather than borrowing the example.

If a warning persists or the indicator behaves unexpectedly, stop the purchasing demonstration and seek qualified advice. Do not ask the seller to erase faults merely to produce a clean handover image. The desired evidence is an explanation and appropriate resolution, not a different photograph of the display.

## History databases cannot reconstruct every repair

The [NMVTIS FAQ](https://vehiclehistory.bja.ojp.gov/faq/list) says that the US system does not include repair history. That matters when a seller presents a title-history report as if it documents airbag replacement. Request the repair records separately and identify which information the report actually supplies.

A vehicle may also have moved between registration markets. Ask what periods the chosen service covers, whether its records identify deployment and whether the workshop named in the invoice can substantiate its own work. Absence of an entry is not evidence that no replacement occurred.

The [FTC's used-car information](https://consumer.ftc.gov/features/feature-0040-used-cars) distinguishes history information from independent inspection. Use the two together. Ask the inspector to review the records before attending so that unexplained replacements receive attention rather than being lost inside a generic condition report.

## Write an inspection brief that can produce a useful answer

State the concern plainly: the car has a disclosed deployment, conflicting repair records, undocumented replacement parts, or an abnormal warning indication. Ask the assessor to identify the vehicle, the material reviewed, the applicable technical information and the limits of the inspection. Request a written conclusion about the questions they could address, not an unqualified promise about future crash performance.

Clarify whether additional specialist access or further work is needed. A limited assessment can be useful, but its limits should remain visible. If the seller will not permit the necessary inspection, choose between holding the transaction and selecting a different vehicle. Do not downgrade the required evidence simply because export booking is approaching.

For a multi-vehicle order, record the conclusion separately for each VIN. Avoid declaring the whole batch acceptable because one repaired car has a strong file. Ask for any substitutions to be assessed on their own history and condition.

## Accept a resolution, not a reassurance

A coherent file connects the original concern to identifiable work and a competent assessment. An incomplete file may still leave a safety-critical uncertainty even if the car starts and drives. For a normal road-use purchase, a prudent acceptance condition is resolution of that uncertainty before payment release or handover.

Do not invent a repair budget, assume a replacement part is genuine from its packaging, or infer destination roadworthiness from a foreign workshop invoice. Parts authenticity, repair quality and local eligibility are related but separate questions. Where one remains unresolved, say exactly which one and who is expected to answer it.

## FAQ

### Does an extinguished airbag warning prove a genuine replacement?

No. It is one observed indication. Traceable parts records and qualified inspection address questions that a dashboard image cannot answer.

### Can a history report replace the repair invoice?

No. Check what the report contains. Title-history information may omit repair details, so obtain the work records and assess gaps separately.

### Should a buyer inspect the module personally?

No. Do not dismantle or tamper with an airbag system. Use a qualified workshop and follow the vehicle manufacturer's safety guidance.

### Is this the same as a recall check?

No. This guide evaluates evidence of previous restraint repair and replacement parts. A recall enquiry addresses a different question and does not document the complete repair history.
